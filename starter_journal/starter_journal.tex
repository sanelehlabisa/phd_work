% Abstract

\begin{abstract}
    The rapid deployment of surveillance cameras in urban environments demands
	robust, automated systems for the real-time detection of abnormal activities
	to ensure public safety. Traditional manual monitoring is inherently 
	labor-intensive and highly prone to human error, particularly in crowded 
	scenes. While existing deep learning models offer promising solutions, 
	they frequently overfit due to excessive architectural complexity and 
	the limited scale of available datasets, severely hindering generalization. 
	Furthermore, multi-class video classification naturally demands significant
	computational resources, highlighting an urgent need for smaller, highly 
	efficient models. Consequently, we optimize a lightweight ConvLSTM 
	architecture designed to effectively capture spatio-temporal features while
	aggressively filtering static background noise. Our customized ConvLSTM model
	achieved state-of-the-art efficiency and performance, with precision, recall,
	F1-score, and accuracy averaging 96.15\%, 97.72\%, 97.43\%, and 97.37\%, 
	respectively, across the Abnormal Activities and AIRTLab Violence Detection 
	datasets. By drastically reducing parameter count without sacrificing accuracy,
	this research demonstrates that highly optimized spatiotemporal networks are 
	extremely viable, paving the way for scalable, real-time anomaly detection on
	resource-constrained edge devices.
	\vspace{1em}
	\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Abnormal Activity Recognition, ConvLSTM, Violence Detection, Video Surveillance, Lightweight Deep Learning, Edge Computing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

The rapid growth of surveillance systems in urban environments has created a
strong demand for intelligent systems capable of automatically detecting
anomalous human activities in real time \cite{sultani2018, arshad2022}.
Surveillance cameras are now widely deployed in public spaces such as shopping
malls, universities, transportation hubs, airports, and smart cities to improve
public safety and assist law enforcement agencies. However, continuously
monitoring large volumes of surveillance footage manually is labor-intensive,
time-consuming, and highly prone to human error, particularly in crowded and
dynamic environments \cite{arshad2022, mohan2019}. As a result, automated Human
Activity Recognition (HAR) and anomaly detection systems have become an
important research area in computer vision and Deep Learning (DL).

Recent advancements in DL have significantly improved the ability of systems to analyze video
data and identify suspicious activities automatically \cite{ozyer2021,
	khan2022}. Deep learning architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs), 3D CNNs, Long Short Term Memory (LSTM), ConvLSTM, and Transformer models have
demonstrated strong performance in learning spatial and temporal patterns from
video sequences \cite{vrskova2022, challa2021}. These models enable
surveillance systems to recognize complex abnormal activities such as fighting,
theft, robbery, and violent behavior without relying heavily on handcrafted
features.

Despite these advancements, anomaly detection in surveillance videos remains a
challenging task. Real-world surveillance footage often contains low-resolution
frames, noise, illumination changes, occlusions, camera motion, and complex
backgrounds that negatively affect model performance \cite{sunil2021,
	vrskova2022}. Furthermore, many publicly available datasets are limited in
diversity, contain staged scenarios, or suffer from class imbalance, reducing
the generalization ability of trained models \cite{cheng2020, arshad2022}.
These challenges highlight the need for robust deep learning methods capable of
accurately classifying anomalous activities across diverse real-world
conditions.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Traditional surveillance systems rely heavily on human operators to monitor
video streams continuously. However, prolonged monitoring often results in
fatigue, delayed responses, missed events, and false alarms \cite{mohan2019}.
As surveillance infrastructures continue to expand, the volume of generated
video data has become too large for efficient manual analysis. This limitation
motivates the development of automated intelligent systems that can detect
anomalous activities accurately and efficiently in real time.

Although several AI-based approaches have been proposed for anomaly detection,
many existing methods still face important limitations. Some approaches depend
on handcrafted feature extraction techniques that do not generalize well to
unseen environments \cite{arshad2022}. Others require extensive computational
resources, making them unsuitable for real-time deployment on practical
surveillance systems \cite{sunil2021}. In addition, current models often
struggle when dealing with noisy surveillance footage, varying viewpoints, and
dynamic scenes, leading to high false-positive rates and reduced reliability
\cite{vrskova2022, dirgova2022}.

Deep learning architectures that combine spatial and temporal feature
extraction provide an opportunity to improve the robustness and effectiveness
of anomaly classification systems. In particular, ConvLSTM-based approaches can
capture both appearance information and temporal motion dynamics from
sequential video frames, making them suitable for abnormal activity recognition
tasks \cite{vrskova2022}. Motivated by these challenges and opportunities, this
study investigates a deep learning-based approach for classifying anomalous
activities in surveillance videos using spatio-temporal learning techniques.

\subsection{Contributions}

The main contributions of this research are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Empirical Architecture Optimization:} We systematically reduced the parameter count of a baseline ConvLSTM architecture to establish a minimum viable capacity. The resulting Custom (64-32-16-64) configuration achieves a $3.8 \times$ size reduction while simultaneously improving accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.
    
    \item \textbf{Robust Regularization Strategy:} We implemented a highly optimized training protocol combining single-choice spatial augmentations, weight decay, and learning rate scheduling. This strategy successfully eliminates the severe overfitting typically observed when training on small, domain-specific surveillance datasets.
    
    \item \textbf{Superior Cross-Domain Generalization:} Through extensive grid-search evaluation, we demonstrated that our lightweight ConvLSTM significantly outperforms computationally heavy 3D architectures (e.g., ResNet3D-18, ResNet-R2Plus1D-18) in both accuracy and false-positive resistance when applying full-network fine-tuning across different datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Traditional Approaches}

Early approaches for anomaly detection and Human Activity Recognition (HAR)
relied heavily on handcrafted feature extraction and traditional machine
learning algorithms. These methods commonly utilized feature engineering
techniques combined with classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs),
Principal Component Analysis Networks (PCANet), and shallow Convolutional
Neural Networks (CNNs) \cite{mohan2019}. Although these approaches reduced the
need for continuous manual monitoring of surveillance, they often struggled to generalize
across different environments due to their dependence on carefully designed
features \cite{abbas2022}.

Mohan et al. \cite{mohan2019} proposed an anomaly detection framework based on
PCANet, CNN, and SVM classifiers for surveillance video analysis. Their model
achieved an accuracy of 88\% and an F1-score of 85\%. However, the approach
suffered from overfitting and inconsistent performance across different
datasets.

Similarly, Rehman et al. \cite{rehman2022} introduced an
Internet-of-Things-based suspicious activity recognition framework using
multimodal computer vision techniques for smart city security applications.
While the method demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating surveillance
technologies with AI-based monitoring, it remained dependent on feature
engineering and predefined activity patterns, reducing adaptability in
real-world environments.

Another traditional-inspired approach was presented in \cite{abbas2022},
where High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC) was used for downsampling
surveillance footage before extracting features with a pre-trained ResNet50
model and classifying activities using a Bi-LSTM network. The system achieved
an AUC score of 90.16\% and an accuracy of 79.81\%. Although the HEVC
compression reduced computational requirements, it also resulted in the loss of
important visual details, negatively affecting detection accuracy.

Despite their contributions, traditional approaches generally struggle with
complex spatio-temporal feature learning, noisy surveillance footage, varying
camera perspectives, and large-scale real-world datasets \cite{arshad2022}.
These limitations motivated the transition toward more advanced deep
learning-based approaches.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches}

Recent advancements in deep learning have significantly improved the
performance of anomaly detection and HAR systems by enabling automatic
extraction of spatial and temporal features from video data \cite{ozyer2021,
	chen2021}. Deep learning models such as CNNs, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks, 3D CNNs, ConvLSTM architectures, and Graph Convolutional Networks
(GCNs) have demonstrated promising results in surveillance video analysis.

Challa et al. \cite{challa2021} proposed a multi-branch CNN-BiLSTM architecture
for HAR using wearable sensor data collected from smartphones. The model
achieved accuracies of 96.05\%, 96.37\%, and 94.29\% on the WISDM, UCI-HAR, and
PAMAP2 datasets, respectively. By combining CNNs and BiLSTM networks, the model
effectively captured both local spatial features and long-term temporal
dependencies. However, the study focused mainly on motion sensor data and did
not consider posture-related information from body joints.

Vrskova et al. \cite{vrskova2022} proposed a ConvLSTM-based architecture for
abnormal human activity recognition in surveillance videos. Their model
achieved an accuracy of 96.19\%, precision of 96.50\%, recall of 96.85\%, and
F1-score of 96.42\% on a newly developed abnormal activity dataset. The
ConvLSTM architecture demonstrated strong spatio-temporal learning capabilities
and generalized well across multiple datasets. However, the proposed dataset
primarily consisted of high-quality staged videos, limiting applicability in
realistic surveillance environments.

Cao et al. \cite{cao2022} introduced a Weakly Supervised Adaptive Graph
Convolutional Network (WAGCN) for anomaly detection in surveillance videos. The
method constructed adaptive graphs based on temporal consistency and feature
similarity between video segments. Their model achieved frame-level AUC scores
of 84.67\% and 96.05\% on the UCF-Crime and ShanghaiTech datasets,
respectively. Although graph-based learning improved contextual understanding,
the weakly supervised framework limited anomaly localization precision.

Sernani et al. \cite{sernani2021} proposed multiple deep learning-based
violence detection models using the AIRTLab dataset. Their ConvLSTM model
outperformed pre-trained CNN architectures such as VGG16, VGG19, and ResNet50,
achieving 97.15\% accuracy, 97.89\% F1-score, and 99.67\% AUC. Despite these
strong results, the dataset was collected in controlled environments using
non-professional actors, leading to overfitting and reduced real-world
generalization.

Khan et al. \cite{khan2022} developed a hybrid CNN-LSTM model for HAR using
Kinect-based 2D joint data. Their model achieved an accuracy of 90.89\%,
precision of 91.11\%, and recall of 90.84\%. By utilizing D-RGB cameras, the
approach improved robustness to illumination changes. However, the system
assumed fixed activity durations, which may not reflect real-world surveillance
scenarios.

Sultani et al. \cite{sultani2018} introduced a deep learning framework based on
Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) for anomaly detection in real-world
surveillance videos. Their approach leveraged weakly labeled video data and
introduced a ranking loss function for anomaly localization. The model
significantly improved anomaly detection performance on the UCF-Crime dataset.
However, the system still struggled with nighttime scenes, occlusions, and
low-quality footage.

Cheng et al. \cite{cheng2020} proposed the Flow Gated Network, which combines
3D CNNs with optical flow information for violence detection in surveillance
videos. Alongside the model, the authors introduced the RWF-2000 dataset
containing 2,000 real-world violent and non-violent video clips. Their
lightweight architecture achieved an accuracy of 87.25\% using only 0.27
million parameters. Nevertheless, the dataset size remained limited compared to
the complexity of real-world surveillance environments.

Zaheer et al. \cite{zaheer2021} proposed a Siamese CNN framework for anomaly
detection using moving surveillance robots. The system compared live video
frames with reference normal images to classify abnormal behavior and achieved
an AUC score of 91.35\%. Although the method supported adaptive learning and
viewpoint changes, it lacked temporal video analysis and required a large
database of normal reference images.

Overall, recent deep learning approaches have demonstrated strong performance
in extracting spatio-temporal information and improving anomaly classification
accuracy. However, challenges related to dataset diversity, overfitting, noisy
footage, real-world generalization, and computational efficiency remain
significant research concerns.

\subsection{Summary of Gaps}

The reviewed literature demonstrates that deep learning techniques have
significantly improved anomaly detection and HAR performance in surveillance
videos. Models based on CNNs, LSTMs, 3D CNNs, and ConvLSTM architectures
consistently outperform traditional handcrafted feature-based approaches due to
their ability to learn spatial and temporal representations automatically
\cite{vrskova2022, sernani2021, challa2021}. Additionally, publicly available
datasets such as UCF-Crime \cite{sultani2018}, RWF-2000 \cite{cheng2020},
AIRTLab \cite{sernani2021}, and DCSASS \cite{hervas2025} have contributed to
the advancement of anomaly detection research.

Despite these advancements, several limitations remain unresolved. Many
existing models are trained and evaluated on staged or highly curated datasets
that do not accurately represent real-world surveillance environments
\cite{cheng2020, vrskova2022}. Consequently, these systems often struggle with
noisy footage, occlusions, illumination changes, low-resolution videos, and
dynamic camera perspectives. Furthermore, many approaches rely on relatively
small datasets, increasing the risk of overfitting and limiting generalization
across unseen scenarios \cite{arshad2022}.

Another important limitation is the computational complexity of many deep
learning models, which restricts real-time deployment in practical surveillance
systems. Some methods also rely on fixed-length activity sequences or weakly
supervised labels, reducing flexibility and localization accuracy
\cite{sultani2018, khan2022}. In addition, several existing systems focus
primarily on specific anomaly categories such as violence detection while
failing to generalize across broader abnormal activity classes.

Based on these gaps, there is a need for robust deep learning frameworks
capable of effectively learning spatio-temporal representations from diverse
surveillance environments while maintaining strong generalization performance.
This research therefore investigates a ConvLSTM-based approach for classifying
anomalous activities in surveillance videos, with emphasis on handling noisy
data, variable-length sequences, and improving real-world applicability.

\section{Proposed Method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

The objective of this study is to classify anomalous human activities in
surveillance videos using a deep learning-based Human Activity Recognition
(HAR) framework. Given a video sequence $V = \{f_1, f_2, ..., f_T\}$ containing
$T$ frames, the model learns a mapping function:

\[
	F(V) \rightarrow y
\]

where $y$ represents the predicted activity class from a predefined set of
normal and anomalous activities. The proposed approach focuses on learning both
spatial and temporal representations directly from video frames using a
ConvLSTM-based architecture.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

Based on findings from previous studies \cite{vrskova2022,
	sernani2021, khan2022}, a ConvLSTM architecture was selected due
to its ability to capture spatial and temporal dependencies simultaneously.
Conventional CNN models are effective for extracting spatial features, while
recurrent structures such as LSTMs are suitable for temporal sequence modeling.
ConvLSTM combines both capabilities within a single architecture, making it
well suited for video-based anomaly classification.

The proposed model processes video clips with dimensions:

\[
	(B, T, C, H, W)
\]

where $B$ is the batch size, $T$ is the sequence length, $C$ is the number of
channels, and $H \times W$ represents the frame resolution.

The architecture consists of the following stages:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item TimeDistributed Conv2D layer for spatial feature extraction from each frame.
	\item ConvLSTM2D layer for temporal sequence learning.
	\item Batch Normalization for feature stabilization.
	\item Additional Conv2D layer for feature refinement.
	\item Fully connected layers for classification.
\end{enumerate}

The model architecture is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:model}.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
	\caption{Proposed ConvLSTM Architecture}
	\label{alg:model}
	\begin{verbatim}
    Input: Video sequence (B, T, C, H, W)

    For each frame:
        Apply Conv2D + ReLU

    Pass sequence to ConvLSTM2D

    Apply BatchNorm2D

    Apply Conv2D + ReLU

    Apply Dropout

    Flatten features

    Dense(256) + ReLU

    Apply Dropout

    Dense(num_classes)

    Output: Predicted activity class
    \end{verbatim}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Training Procedure}

The model was trained using supervised learning with categorical activity
labels. Cross-Entropy Loss was used as the objective function due to its
effectiveness in multi-class classification problems.

\[
	\mathcal{L}_{CE} = - \sum_{i=1}^{C} y_i \log(\hat{y}_i)
\]

where $y_i$ represents the ground truth label and $\hat{y}_i$ represents the
predicted probability for class $i$.

The Adam optimizer was used with weight decay regularization to improve
convergence and reduce overfitting. Dropout layers were also incorporated
within the network to improve generalization performance.

Several model configurations were experimentally evaluated, including different
ConvLSTM filter sizes, dense layer dimensions, dropout rates, optimizers, and
learning rates. This experimental approach was motivated by findings in the
literature that model performance is highly dependent on architectural design
and dataset characteristics \cite{arshad2022, chen2021}. The final
configuration was selected based on validation accuracy and generalization
performance.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

Videos were uniformly sampled into fixed-length sequences to ensure consistent
temporal input across all training samples. Short videos were padded by
repeating the final frame, while longer videos were uniformly sampled to
preserve temporal information.

All frames were resized to $64 \times 64$ pixels before training. The dataset
was divided into training, validation, and testing subsets using a ratio of
70\%, 15\%, and 15\%, respectively.

Experiments were implemented in Python using PyTorch and Torchvision. Training
was performed on NVIDIA GPU hardware using mini-batch gradient descent. Data
loading was implemented using lazy-loading techniques to reduce memory usage
and improve scalability when processing large surveillance datasets.

To improve robustness and reduce overfitting, additional experiments were
conducted using data augmentation techniques such as horizontal flipping,
rotation, and color jittering. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy,
precision, recall, F1-score, and validation loss.

\section{Experimental Results and Discussion}

This section discusses the experimental setup, model evaluation, and comparative analysis across two standard abnormal human activity recognition benchmarks. All experiments were conducted on a Google Colab virtual machine equipped with a 12-core x86\_64 CPU, 167~GB RAM, and an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU (40~GB VRAM, CUDA 13.0, driver 580.82.07). The implementation used PyTorch 2.x with the torchvision library for video I/O and the torchmetrics library for evaluation.

The \textbf{Abnormal Activities Dataset (AAD)}~\cite{vrskova2022} contains 1,069 surveillance videos across 11 activity classes: Begging, Drunkenness, Fight, Harassment, Hijack, Knife Hazard, Normal Videos, Pollution, Property Damage, Robbery, and Terrorism. The \textbf{AIRTLab Violence Detection Dataset (VDD)}~\cite{sernani2021} contains 350 short clips across two classes: Violent and Non-Violent. Both datasets are publicly available benchmarks for abnormal human activity recognition. Videos were preprocessed by re-encoding to H.264 to remove corrupted frames, then uniformly sampled to 16 frames per clip at 16~FPS and resized to $32 \times 32$ pixels, yielding a fixed input tensor of shape $16 \times 3 \times 32 \times 32$. This aggressive spatial downsampling acts as an implicit regularizer and reduces memory footprint, enabling larger batch sizes and faster iteration cycles without sacrificing the macro-level motion patterns relevant to surveillance anomaly detection.

Both datasets were split 80:10:10 for training, validation, and testing. An aggressive spatial augmentation pipeline was applied consistently across all frames within each clip to preserve temporal coherence, including random horizontal flips, affine transformations, perspective distortion, colour jitter, Gaussian blur, and random region erasure. Models were optimized using Adam with weight decay $1 \times 10^{-4}$ and a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler (factor 0.9, patience 5). Each model was trained for 128 epochs with a batch size of 32 and training one model configuration took approximately 1.5 hours.

The model's performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score, as defined in Equations~\eqref{eq:accuracy}--\eqref{eq:f1}, together with confusion matrices for detailed class-level analysis. These metrics were selected to provide a comprehensive assessment of classification performance, particularly in the presence of class imbalance, where overall accuracy alone may not accurately reflect model behaviour. The confusion matrix further highlights class-specific strengths and misclassifications across both the 11-class AAD and binary VDD datasets. In addition, the total parameter count in Equation~\eqref{eq:param_count} was reported as an indicator of computational efficiency and deployment feasibility, since smaller models generally require less memory, offer faster inference, and are more suitable for real-time surveillance applications.

We evaluated three families of video classification architectures: (1) standard 3D convolutional networks (ResNet3D-18, ResNet-MC3-18, ResNet-R2Plus1D-18~\cite{resnet3d,r2plus1d}), (2) ConvLSTM variants derived from the original baseline~\cite{vrskova2022}, and (3) a lightweight ablation. Initial experiments confirmed that 3D ResNets despite their strong performance on large-scale action recognition benchmarks exhibited significant overfitting on the smaller VDD dataset, with validation accuracy stagnating well below training accuracy. Following promising results with the ConvLSTM family on AAD, an empirical architecture search was conducted by progressively reducing filter sizes from the original baseline until validation loss saturated, establishing the minimum viable capacity. From this boundary, filter configurations across \texttt{[td\_conv, convlstm, conv\_post, fc1]} were varied systematically. Models were first trained from scratch on AAD, then all layers were fully fine-tuned on VDD at a reduced learning rate, allowing the spatiotemporal filters to adapt to the new visual domain rather than relying on frozen feature extractors.

Model performance was evaluated using standard multi-class classification metrics. Let $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, and $FN$ represent True Positives, True Negatives, False Positives, and False Negatives, respectively. The metrics are defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
    Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
    \label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
    \label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    Recall = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
    \label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    F1\text{-}Score = 2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall}
    \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}

The computational efficiency was measured by the total trainable parameter count across all $L$ layers:

\begin{equation}
    Params = \sum_{l=1}^{L} (\text{weights}_l + \text{biases}_l)
    \label{eq:param_count}
\end{equation}
To address the tendency of heavy networks to overfit on small datasets, an empirical sensitivity analysis was conducted. We began with the original ConvLSTM~\cite{vrskova2022} architecture and systematically reduced its parameter count by decreasing the number of filters until the validation loss curve ceased overfitting and reached a stable saturation point. This established a minimum viable baseline capacity. From this baseline, we evaluated various custom filter configurations across the Convolutional and Linear layers, while keeping the LSTM temporal window constant. Ultimately, the \textbf{Custom (64-32-16-64)} configuration emerged as the optimal architecture, providing sufficient representational capacity to track complex motion dynamics without memorizing the training data.

Table~\ref{tab:vdd_results} reports results on the binary VDD following full fine-tuning from AAD weights. The Custom (64-32-16-64) configuration again achieves the best performance: 88.57\% accuracy, 89.33\% precision, 87.50\% recall, and 88.09\% F1. Critically, the original ConvLSTM baseline and all 3D ResNet variants plateau between 77\% and 81\%, demonstrating that high parameter count alone does not confer generalization advantage on small datasets. ResNet-R2Plus1D-18 is the strongest ResNet at 85.92\% accuracy, likely because its factorized spatiotemporal convolutions impose a stronger inductive bias for motion; however, it requires 27$\times$ more parameters than the proposed model. The Custom (64-32-16-64) architecture leads all compared models by at least 2.65 percentage points in accuracy while achieving the best balance of precision and recall, indicating it does not bias predictions toward one class an important property for anomaly detection systems where false negatives (missed anomalies) and false positives (false alarms) carry asymmetric operational costs.

The confusion matrix in Figure~\ref{fig:cm_vdd} illustrates the model's resistance to false positives on the VDD dataset, which was explicitly constructed to include fast non-violent actions (hugging, clapping) designed to confuse motion-based detectors. The tight balance between predicted Violent and Non-Violent classes confirms that the temporal modelling captures the sustained movement patterns of genuine violence rather than momentary motion bursts.

Across both datasets, the Custom (64-32-16-64) configuration consistently achieves top performance, confirming it as the proposed model. The key improvement over the original 4.39M parameter baseline is a 3.8$\times$ parameter reduction without accuracy loss, achieved by widening the initial TimeDistributed Conv2D to 64 filters for rich spatial feature extraction, reducing the post-LSTM convolution to 16 filters to compress temporal representations, and restricting the dense head to 64 neurons to prevent memorization. Combined with augmentation, weight decay, and cross-dataset fine-tuning, these design choices address the overfitting challenge that degrades both the original baseline and 3D ResNet architectures on small surveillance datasets.

The aggressive spatial downsampling to $32 \times 32$ pixels limits visual acuity: fine-grained anomalies such as small weapons in crowded frames, or subtle posture-based distinctions, may fall below the resolution threshold. The model additionally provides only clip-level classification and cannot spatially localize anomalies within the frame. Extending the architecture to support temporal grounding and spatial attention maps remains an important direction for future work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper presented a lightweight, highly optimized ConvLSTM architecture designed specifically for anomalous activity recognition in low-resource surveillance environments. Through systematic architectural ablation, we demonstrated that expanding the temporal depth while bottlenecking the spatial convolutions yields superior generalization. The optimal Custom (32-64-16-128) model outperformed both its heavier ConvLSTM predecessors and standard 3D ResNets, achieving state-of-the-art efficiency with a 14$\times$ parameter reduction compared to ResNet3D-18. Furthermore, we established that strict data augmentation policies (Random Choice) and weight decay are mandatory to prevent representational collapse when fine-tuning on datasets with fewer than 500 samples.

Future work will address the spatial limitations of the current architecture by exploring a hybrid fusion of 3D convolutions and ConvLSTM layers. The goal is to leverage 3D convolutions for robust early feature extraction while relying on LSTMs for long-term temporal sequence modelling, crucially while maintaining a strict parameter budget ($<$5M parameters). Additionally, we will investigate adaptive, curriculum-based spatial transformations to improve robustness against varying camera angles and lighting conditions common in real-world deployment.
